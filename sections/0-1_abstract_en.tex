Two networks can learn the same arithmetic equally well and use very
different amounts of themselves to do it. On addition modulo a prime, a layer
of $256$ units carries the solution on about $154$ of them at a test accuracy
of $0.998$. Alter one property of the update, which parameters share a
normalising factor, and the same solution occupies about $254$ units at
$0.976$, with the problem itself untouched. Widen the layer to $2048$ and the
two settings use $332$ units and $2046$, both at $0.998$. The arithmetic a
network is asked to compute therefore leaves how much of the network does the
computing open, and the way training divides the error among the neurons
settles it.

The question comes from a survey of $87$ agents for computer use and $33$
datasets. Specialised agents are giving way to agents built on pretrained
foundation models, while how those agents learn and what they observe are
unsettled, and six limitations recur across the field. Three of the six are about evaluation and
deployment, and a fourth, planning over many steps, the survey attributes
partly to the model and partly to an environment nobody has characterised. Two concern what training produced,
namely that these systems generalise poorly beyond the situations they were
trained on and need large amounts of demonstration data.
A survey cannot explain those two, because it never holds a task fixed and
varies how a network is trained, and because a success rate cannot separate
two networks that score alike. Both call for a measurement taken inside the
network itself.

Doing that needs a task whose solution is known neuron by neuron, and
arithmetic modulo a prime is such a task. Networks trained on it grok: they fit the
training set early, then spend thousands of epochs no better than chance on
unseen pairs before generalising all at once. Holding
the task, the data, the architecture and the width fixed and varying only the
rule that assigns credit, every rule that generalises recovers the same
Fourier construction, and the number of neurons implementing it differs by
more than half again between rules.

A single condition accounts for the spread between the rules. A unit
survives training when its credit outweighs the non gradient part of its own
update, and that criterion subsumes three mechanisms previously described one
at a time: an L2 term that reaches the weights through the gradient, the small
constant that keeps an adaptive optimizer's denominator from vanishing, and a
trainable gain that decays away. A normalising factor shared by exactly one
neuron is the only part of an update able to shift that ratio, which separates
the optimizers in general use from the two rules that carry such a factor.
Equalising the credit neurons receive, while leaving
every gradient's direction unchanged, moves the computation from $60$ to $99$
per cent of the layer, at a cost of two points of test accuracy, $0.998$ to
$0.976$. Imposing a
credit profile directly, with a random permutation deciding which neuron
receives which magnitude, moves it the same way, which excludes an account in
which the credit merely tracked what the neurons were already doing. The
effect grows with the width of the layer and vanishes where the
untouched network already uses all of it.

The choice carries a price at each end of its range. A computation spread over many
neurons tolerates the deletion of neurons far better. A computation held on
few prunes further and reaches a task that needs new combinations of the
features already learned in $640$ epochs against $1540$ for the spread one,
whose $1540$ about matches the $1410$ that training from scratch needs. The
advantage is a narrow one. It disappears when a target keeps the same features
and merely reassigns their votes, and on multiplication training from scratch
beats every pretrained network.

Other components of the training setup leave structure behind as well. Hold
the credit assignment algorithm fixed and vary the training objective, and a
Transformer's attention weights accumulate a sum restricted to
whichever ordered token pairs that objective admits. Under bidirectional training every pair
arrives in both orders and the weights stay near symmetric. Under
autoregressive training a token is a target once and context repeatedly, and
the weights turn directional: high norm columns start to outnumber high norm
rows. The derivation holds in matched
twelve layer models and the structure appears in released models across text,
vision and audio. An initialisation that follows from the derivation speeds up
bidirectional training.

Every measurement of a trained network depends on the readout that produced
it, this thesis's own measurement included. The standard probe for a latent world model is
fitted on encoded observations and applied to predicted ones, and when the training objective
scores direction alone, the magnitude is left to drift. Most of what the
probe then reports is that drift. In synthetic data
its error reaches $27$ times that of predicting the average state, and for a
published model rescaling the predictions to the norm of the encodings removes
$97$ per cent of the error without retraining anything. Four checks come with
the correction, and applying them to this thesis's own measure of how far a
computation spreads shows that the direction of the central result survives
while the level it reports is the highest of three measures.

A structure that training settles can also be built into an architecture,
and one architecture did so before any of this was measured. The last study, submitted
two years before the work above, builds a sensory pattern out of neurons that
hold one another in place, assembled from fragments that Hebbian plasticity
learns from data. Noise that nothing supports is filtered out with more than
half the input corrupted, partially hidden figures are completed, and
fragments learned separately recombine into patterns never seen in training.
The model has so far been demonstrated only on line drawings, so the thesis
closes by setting out what a
representation of this kind would need in order to serve as the state of an
agent's model of its environment.
